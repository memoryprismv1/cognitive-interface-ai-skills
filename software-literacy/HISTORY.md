# History: Software Literacy for AI Collaboration

This file records the development of the `software-literacy` skill. It distinguishes the user's motivating observations, external research, design decisions, and work still to be done. It is not a claim that the proposed skill has been validated.

## 6 November 2026 — The problem identified

The user proposed a new skill for people without software-engineering backgrounds who are using AI to create or modify software. The motivating concern is that many people can ask AI to produce software without understanding what the AI has generated, what its explanations mean, what choices are being made, or what consequences those choices may have.

The user also identified a communication problem: AI assistants frequently use software terminology that even an experienced, non-specialist collaborator may find difficult to translate. The assistant must therefore take responsibility for explaining its own technical language.

The purpose is not to teach everyone to become a software engineer. It is to make people better informed and more aware of their choices, while making AI-assisted software work more understandable.

The initial working name was **Software Literacy for AI Collaboration**, with the folder and skill identifier `software-literacy`.

## 6 November 2026 — Research directions identified

The project identified four connected research strands:

1. AI-generated code quality, security, technical debt, and maintainability.
2. Novice and non-specialist use of AI coding tools, including product–understanding gaps.
3. Comprehension, cognitive offloading, learning, and automation bias.
4. Verification, human oversight, and informed decision-making.

The first research set includes studies of AI-authored commits in public repositories, comparisons of human- and AI-generated code, a randomized trial with software developers learning a new library, and an experiment measuring code ownership among a mixed group of students, researchers, and professional developers.

An important limitation became clear: these studies do not directly establish the extent of the problem among people with no software background who use AI agents to build real applications. This remains a specific research gap.

## 6 November 2026 — The user's prior work and intellectual provenance

The user noted that they had already written about these problems on the Memory Prism website and wanted their work included in the intellectual foundation.

The following relevant essays were located and their exact URLs verified:

- [The Intern Who Never Sleeps](https://www.memoryprism.com/positions/ai-intern-monkeys) — the mismatch between AI generation and human verification capacity.
- [AEL and AI](https://www.memoryprism.com/positions/ael-and-ai) — capability without consequence and the need to account for consequences.
- [Vive et Vivere Sinas](https://www.memoryprism.com/essays/signal-and-cost/vive-et-vivere-sinas) — consequence literacy, continuity, and education as updating consequence maps.

These are part of the user's existing intellectual work. The skill operationalises relevant ideas for AI-assisted software collaboration. External research may support, challenge, or independently converge with those arguments; it must not be presented as their origin.

## Design decisions

- The skill is an AI behaviour skill, not a conventional software course.
- Teach software concepts in context, when they become relevant to the user's task.
- Explain the actual technical term as well as its plain-language meaning.
- Preserve the user's agency over consequential choices.
- Distinguish generation, execution, testing, passing a test, and verification.
- Explain trade-offs, dependencies, risks, and reversibility.
- Do not patronise people or confuse lack of technical vocabulary with lack of reasoning ability.
- Keep research findings separate from the proposed intervention and its untested outcomes.
- Coordinate with existing Cognitive Interface skills rather than duplicating them.

## Open work

- Expand the research review, especially studies involving people with little or no software background.
- Verify full publication details and inspect source papers before adding stronger claims.
- Develop an evaluation protocol using practical tasks and comprehension measures, not self-reported confidence alone.
- Test the skill with people from different levels of software experience.
- Record results, including negative or mixed findings, without overstating success.

## Evidence boundary

The initial design reflects the user's stated problem, the user's published Memory Prism work, and external research leads inspected during development. It has not yet been evaluated with users. Its effectiveness remains an open empirical question.
