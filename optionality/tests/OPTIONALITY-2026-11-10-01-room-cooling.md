# Optionality Test 01: Cooling a Room Without Air-Conditioning

**Date:** 2026-11-10  
**Skill:** Optionality  
**Test type:** Open-ended design problem; alternative generation and preservation  
**Prompt selected by assistant:** How could someone reduce heat in a room without installing air-conditioning?

## Test intent

Test whether Optionality produces genuinely different approaches rather than cosmetic variations, develops the branches enough to expose their trade-offs, and avoids forcing the user to evaluate or select all of them.

This is a qualitative, assistant-selected probe. It is not a controlled skill-on/skill-off comparison.

## Possibility space

The problem can be approached at three different points in the heat balance. These are not three brands of the same solution.

### Branch A — Stop heat entering

Treat the room as an envelope problem.

- Shade the outside of sun-facing windows before sunlight reaches the glass.
- Reduce heat transfer through the roof or ceiling, where feasible.
- Seal obvious gaps that admit hot outdoor air during the hottest hours.
- Use curtains or reflective internal coverings as a secondary measure where exterior shading is impractical.

**What this changes:** The room gains less heat in the first place.

**Trade-offs:** Exterior shading and roof work can be more effective but may require installation, landlord approval, or higher upfront effort. Internal curtains are easier but cannot prevent all solar heat from entering the room.

### Branch B — Remove heat already inside

Treat the room as an airflow and timing problem.

- Ventilate when outdoor air is cooler than indoor air, often at night or early morning.
- Create a cross-flow path with openings on different sides if the building layout allows it.
- Use an exhaust fan to push hot air out, with a planned inlet for replacement air.
- Reduce internal heat sources during the hot part of the day.

**What this changes:** The room exchanges hot indoor air for cooler outdoor air when the temperature difference makes that useful.

**Trade-offs:** Ventilation can make the room hotter if outdoor air is hotter, and it may bring dust, noise, humidity, or security concerns. Fans move air; they do not lower the air temperature by themselves.

### Branch C — Cool the occupied zone, not the whole room

Treat the problem as one of human exposure rather than whole-room temperature.

- Direct a fan across the occupied area.
- Use breathable bedding and clothing, and reduce heat-trapping fabrics.
- Move work or rest to the coolest part of the room or to a shaded room during peak heat.
- Use a small, local evaporative cooling method only where humidity and ventilation make it suitable.

**What this changes:** It can improve comfort without requiring the entire room's air and surfaces to become cool.

**Trade-offs:** It may not protect heat-sensitive equipment, pets, or stored items elsewhere in the room. Evaporative methods work poorly in humid conditions and can add moisture.

## A fourth route — Change the room's operating schedule

This is a hybrid strategy rather than another cooling device:

- Keep sun-facing openings shaded during the day.
- Keep windows closed when outdoor air is hotter, if the room is otherwise reasonably sealed.
- Flush the room with cooler air when outdoor conditions improve.
- Avoid adding avoidable heat during the hottest hours.

It combines parts of A and B, but its distinctive contribution is sequencing: the same window can be useful at one time and harmful at another.

## Why these are materially different

| Route | Main intervention point | Main benefit | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Stop heat entering | Building envelope | Reduces heat gain | May need installation |
| B. Remove heat | Air exchange | Clears accumulated heat | Depends on outdoor conditions |
| C. Cool occupied zone | Local comfort | Lower effort and targeted relief | Does not cool the whole room |
| D. Change schedule | Timing and operation | Coordinates the other routes | Requires conditions-aware operation |

These routes can be combined. They are not mutually exclusive choices.

## Optionality assessment

- **Distinctness:** Pass. The branches intervene at different points: heat entry, heat removal, personal exposure, and timing.
- **Development:** Pass. Each branch includes mechanism, benefit, and constraints rather than being only a label.
- **Preservation without forced choice:** Pass. The branches can be combined; no ranking or selection was demanded.
- **Restraint:** Pass with a caveat. Four branches were enough to reveal different mechanisms. A longer list of gadgets would have added little.
- **Evidence discipline:** Partial. This is a conceptual design map, not a measured comparison. Actual effectiveness depends on climate, humidity, building orientation, insulation, security, and room layout.
- **User agency:** Not tested. No user selected, rejected, combined, or deferred a branch in this probe.

## Limitations

The assistant selected the problem and generated and assessed its own alternatives. There was no skill-disabled baseline, blind evaluator, measured temperature comparison, or user feedback. This tests whether the skill's operating pattern can be demonstrated in one response; it does not establish improved user outcomes or general reliability.
