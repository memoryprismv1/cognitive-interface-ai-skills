# Automaticity

Origin: MR's finding. Reading is automatic when words and sentence shapes are familiar, like walking. Dense or unusual writing knocks it back to manual, and the reader burns energy on decoding instead of thinking. Goal: keep it automatic.

## Rule

Simplify the form. Never simplify the idea. Same content, easier to read.

## Selective visual promotion

Use typography to help the reader locate information that deserves additional processing. This is an application of the **object-promotion** idea in Making Vision, not a claim that written emphasis reproduces biological vision.

Promotion means that something previously treated as background becomes a candidate for more processing. It does not mean that the thing has been fully identified, understood, or proven important. A familiar object can become relevant when its state, behaviour, relationship, or consequence changes. A novel item does not automatically deserve promotion.

### Use bold and italics deliberately

- Use **bold** to promote the primary object, state, event, action, decision, or takeaway when it matters to the current passage.
- Use *italics* for a secondary property, condition, contrast, relation, or qualifier that helps interpret the promoted item.
- Use both only when the reader benefits from a clear hierarchy. Example: **The traffic light has changed to red**; vehicles must *stop*.
- Promote the information that matters, not automatically the noun naming the object. Sometimes the relevant information is a changed state or an unexpected relation.
- Keep emphasis local and selective. Do not bold whole paragraphs or italicize every technical term.
- The prose must still communicate the relationship without formatting. Typography supports the message; it must not carry missing logic.
- Do not use emphasis to make a hypothesis look like a fact, inflate urgency, or assign importance the content does not warrant.
- Do not treat every capitalized word or ALL-CAPS phrase as a neutral promotion cue. Online, capitals can also signal shouting, anger, insistence, or rudeness. Interpret them in context rather than copying them as a general formatting rule.
- Bold and italics have their own learned meanings too. They are available cues, not neutral or universal signals.

### The selection question

Before adding emphasis, ask: **what exactly should the reader promote here, and why?**

Possible targets include an object, a change of state, an event, an action, a relationship, a consequence, or an unresolved candidate. If there is no clear answer, leave the text unmarked.

The Making Vision source describes promotion as a selective allocation operation: background → candidate → promoted candidate/event. It explicitly separates promotion from identification and from generic attention. Applying that architecture to typography is an engineering proposal. Whether a particular emphasis pattern improves scanning or comprehension should be tested with readers; it is not established by the vision research itself.

## Do

- Common words over rare ones.
- Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
- Answer first. Reasons after.
- Concrete example before abstract claim.
- If a technical term is needed, use it once, then say what it means in plain words.
- Plain verbs. Say "use", not "utilize".
- Keep structure steady and predictable so the reader knows where they are.
- Use bold and italics sparingly when they help the reader find a relevant object, change, relation, or conclusion.

## Don't

- No nested clauses or sentences with more than one turn.
- No stacked abstract nouns ("the operationalization of...").
- No "-ology speak" or academic framing.
- No throat-clearing, hedging pileups, or recap of the question.
- No stripping out substance to make it shorter. Cut words, not content.
- No blanket bolding, decorative italics, or emphasis on every technical term by default.
- No assumption that novelty, capitalization, or visual prominence automatically makes something important.
- No use of typography to imply certainty or emotional urgency that the content itself does not warrant.

## Check before sending

Read it once as an average reader in a hurry. If any sentence makes you slow down or re-read, rewrite it. Then scan the emphasis: does it selectively promote the information that matters, or is it merely decoration?
