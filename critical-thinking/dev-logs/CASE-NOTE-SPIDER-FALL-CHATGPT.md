# Case Note: Can a Spider Survive a Fall from Any Height?

**Assistant:** ChatGPT  
**Inquiry type:** Biomechanics; comparative behaviour; evidence-scope audit  
**Outcome:** The blanket claim is false. Fall outcome varies with body size, posture, silk use, species-specific aerial control, and landing conditions. The available studies do not establish a universal safe height or a universal survival threshold.

## Question

Can a spider survive a fall from any height?

The question sounds like a simple terminal-velocity problem. It is not. It bundles at least three distinct outcomes:

1. How fast does the spider fall?
2. Can it control its descent or landing?
3. Does it survive the impact?

Evidence for one does not automatically answer the others.

For transparency, the following are **post-search hypotheses**, not scored predictions:

- Very small spiders can experience substantial air resistance relative to their weight, but size alone will not determine every fall.
- Dragline silk and body posture can change descent speed.
- Some arboreal spiders may actively steer rather than simply fall.
- Large, heavy-bodied spiders may be vulnerable to impact despite the general intuition that spiders are light.
- A study measuring descent or successful navigation should not be treated as a study of injury or survival unless it measured those outcomes.

## Evidence reviewed

### 1. Free-fall and posture: Suter (1992)

Robert B. Suter's *Journal of Arachnology* study examined spiders trailing known lengths of dragline silk and separately measured drag as living spiders changed posture.

The free-fall sample was very small: five spiders, weighing 0.18, 0.18, 0.20, 1.0 and 1.8 mg. The paper reports substantial variation in terminal velocity. In the drag measurements, relatively small posture changes produced as much as a tenfold change in drag. For one spider at the same silk length, observed terminal velocity varied by a factor of 2.6.

This supports a more careful account: mass and silk matter, but posture can substantially change drag too. The study does **not** establish that every spider has a low terminal speed, nor does it measure survival after impact.

Primary source: Suter, R. B. (1992), “Ballooning: Data from Spiders in Freefall Indicate the Importance of Posture,” *The Journal of Arachnology* 20(3):107–113.  
https://www.americanarachnology.org/journal-joa/joa-all-articles/article/download/JoA_v20_p107.pdf/

### 2. Directed aerial descent: Yanoviak, Munk & Dudley (2015)

The researchers drop-tested 59 canopy-dwelling *Selenops* spiders. Fifty-five (93%) steered their aerial trajectories towards nearby tree trunks and landed successfully. The tested spiders ranged from 2 to 556 mg, with a mean mass of 95 mg. Glide performance decreased as body mass increased.

This is important because it overturns the simple picture of a spider as a passive particle. These spiders actively orient and steer. But the study measured directed descent and landing on tree trunks in a particular ecological setting. It did not establish that 93% of all spiders survive arbitrary falls, or that these animals would survive striking every kind of surface.

Primary source: Yanoviak, S. P., Munk, Y. & Dudley, R. (2015), “Arachnid aloft: directed aerial descent in neotropical canopy spiders,” *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*.  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4614463/

### 3. Impact injury in large spiders

A 2023 review of aquatic and terrestrial invertebrate welfare notes that tarantulas can sustain severe injuries from falls, even short ones. It discusses their open circulatory system: when the exoskeleton is broken, haemolymph loss and secondary infection become risks.

This is a different question from terminal velocity. A spider can descend slowly and still be injured when it hits a hard surface. Body construction and the landing itself matter.

Source: “Aquatic and Terrestrial Invertebrate Welfare,” *Animals* (2023).  
https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/13/21/3375

## Reconciliation

The popular claim appears to combine a valid physical intuition with an invalid universal conclusion.

- **Small body mass** can make air resistance important relative to weight. That can limit fall speed.
- **Posture** changes the area and shape presented to the airflow. Suter's experiments show that this is not a trivial adjustment.
- **Silk** can add drag and can also function as a safety line in some contexts.
- **Some species actively steer.** The *Selenops* drop tests demonstrate directed aerial descent, not just passive slowing.
- **Impact tolerance is separate.** A low fall speed does not guarantee a safe landing, especially for a large, heavy-bodied spider landing on a hard surface.

The phrase “spiders survive falls from any height” is therefore too broad. Even if a spider reaches terminal velocity, that only means its speed has stopped increasing; it does not mean the terminal speed is harmless or that the landing surface cannot injure it.

## Claim boundaries

| Claim | Status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Air resistance can strongly affect the fall of small spiders | **Supported** | Free-fall and drag experiments |
| Posture can change drag substantially | **Supported** | Direct posture/drag measurements |
| All spiders have negligible terminal velocity | **Not supported** | Species and body sizes vary; evidence is not universal |
| Some spiders can steer while falling | **Supported** | Controlled *Selenops* drop tests |
| 93% of spiders survive falls | **False generalisation** | 93% refers to 55 of 59 tested *Selenops* successfully directing descent to tree trunks |
| A spider reaching terminal velocity is guaranteed to survive impact | **Not supported** | Descent speed and impact injury are different outcomes |
| There is a universal safe fall height for spiders | **Not established** | No universal threshold was identified in the evidence reviewed |

## What remains unresolved

- A broad comparative dataset linking spider mass, shape, terminal velocity, landing surface, impact force and injury outcome was not found in this search.
- The *Selenops* study is a strong example of active aerial control, but it cannot stand in for all spiders.
- The welfare review supports fall-related injury risk in tarantulas, but does not provide a general height-versus-injury curve.

A useful next study would measure impact outcomes across species and sizes under controlled, ethically appropriate conditions, while separating passive free-fall, silk-assisted descent and active steering. The existing studies do not justify inventing a universal threshold.
